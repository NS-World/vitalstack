Your email account is the master key to your digital life: every "forgot password" link goes there. If someone gets into your email, they can reset almost everything else. A strong, unique password plus **two-step verification (2FA)** makes your accounts dramatically harder to take over.

::scene The master key
🔑 | One password | Asha uses "Asha@1998" for email, Instagram and a shopping site. | bugsy
💥 | The leak | The shopping site gets hacked. Her email and password are leaked online. | dev
🤖 | The bots | Criminals try that pair on Gmail, Instagram and banks automatically. | chip
🛡️ | The fix | A unique passphrase for each site and 2FA on email stop the attack cold. | byte
::end

## Why simple passwords fail

Attackers don't guess one password at a time. They use software that tries **billions** of combinations and lists of the most common passwords, names, dates and patterns like `Name@123`.

| Password | Why it's weak or strong |
|---|---|
| `123456`, `password` | Top of every leaked-password list: cracked instantly |
| `Rahul@1998` | Name + symbol + birth year is the most common pattern there is |
| `P@ssw0rd!` | Swapping letters for symbols is a trick the cracking tools already know |
| `mango river tiffin 47 kite` | Long, random words: very hard to crack, easy to remember |

**Length beats complexity.** A passphrase of 4–5 random words is stronger than a short password full of symbols.

## The rule that matters most: one password, one account

When any website is hacked, its passwords leak. Criminals then try the same email and password on every popular site, which is called **credential stuffing**. If each account has its own password, one leak stays one leak.

## Password managers

Nobody can remember 50 different passphrases. A **password manager** remembers them for you:

- It creates a long random password for each site and fills it in automatically.
- You remember only one strong master passphrase.
- Many phones and browsers include one (Google Password Manager, iCloud Keychain), and there are dedicated apps.

It also protects you from phishing: the manager won't fill your password on a fake lookalike site.

## Two-step verification (2FA)

With 2FA, logging in needs your password **and** something you have, usually your phone. Even if your password leaks, the attacker is stuck.

| 2FA method | Strength |
|---|---|
| SMS code | Good, much better than nothing |
| Authenticator app (codes that change every 30 seconds) | Better |
| Passkey or security key | Best: cannot be phished |

Turn it on first for: **email, WhatsApp (Settings → Account → Two-step verification), banking apps, Instagram/Facebook and your Google or Apple account.**

## Passkeys: the future without passwords

Many sites now offer **passkeys**: you log in with your phone's fingerprint or face unlock. There is nothing to type, nothing to leak and nothing to phish. If a site offers passkeys, use them.

::tip Check whether your email has appeared in a known data leak at haveibeenpwned.com. If it has, change that password everywhere you reused it, starting with your email account.

## Summary

- Long passphrases of random words beat short "complex" passwords.
- Use a different password for every account; a password manager makes this easy.
- Turn on two-step verification for email, WhatsApp, banking and social media.
- Prefer passkeys and authenticator apps over SMS when available.
